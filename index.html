The root of the Engracia family

In the third quarter of the 17th century an Ottoman missionary and trader, Esau Ozal, sailed from Istanbul to Borneo, married Lileni Mali of Tawi-Tawi, and settled in Zamboanga. Their four orphaned children — renamed Engracia by a Spanish parish priest — became the root of every branch on this tree.

The mission to the Far East

During the third quarter of the 17th century the Supreme Head of the Council for Islamic Affairs under the Ottoman Empire made a plan to send groups of traders, who were at the same time Muslim missionaries, to the Far East to spread the Islamic faith. After months of deliberation two groups were selected for the task, with fifteen men in each. Their voyage took almost a year by sea: from Istanbul through the Mediterranean, down the western and southern coasts of Africa, around the Cape of Good Hope, across the Indian Ocean and through the Strait of Malacca, until they reached the island of Borneo. One group landed on the western side — today's Sarawak, Brunei and Kota Kinabalu — while the other went on to the east, to what are now Sandakan, Kudat and Tawau. Borneo was then a virgin land of countless plants, birds and wild animals, and travel was almost entirely by sea.

One member of the expedition was a young, dashing, adventurous man named ESAU OZAL, leader of the team assigned to eastern Borneo. He was not only a missionary but an expert trader, for the groups had to support themselves financially in their work.

Lileni Mali of Tawi-Tawi

Over many years of missionary travel he reached the islands of Tawi-Tawi — Sitangkai, Sibutu, Balabac — the Sulu Archipelago, Palawan, and as far as Zamboanga. On one of these journeys he met a charming lass from the islands of Tawi-Tawi named Lileni Mali. After a brief courtship they were married in the Muslim ceremonial tradition.

With his plans and vision for a family, Esau brought his wife to Zamboanga and settled there. Trade was prosperous: the goods he carried from Sandakan were in great demand among the local residents and the Spanish employees of the civil government. The family stayed in Zamboanga for a decade and had four children, three boys and a girl — Faisal, Diamla, Istam and Sahala, all surnamed Ozal. Lileni died giving birth to the youngest, Sahala, of a haemorrhage, and Esau became a widower at thirty-five.

The blockade and the orphans

He carried on his missionary work and trading between Zamboanga and Sandakan until Fort Pilar in Zamboanga was attacked by pirates1 and other marauders — not once, but many times. The Spanish government responded by sealing the sea lane between Sandakan, Jolo and Zamboanga. The blockade ruined Esau's trade, and then came the misfortune that shaped the family: he was unable to return to Zamboanga, where his four children had been left behind. They never learned whether their father had died during the strict blockade of the Spanish Navy against native vessels crossing from Borneo and Jolo.

The orphans lived in poverty at the mercy of their neighbours. Faisal, the eldest, was barely ten when his mother died, and at twelve he was the breadwinner for his two brothers and his sister, doing whatever menial work he could find — until he was hired as a helper and assistant cook at the convent of the local Spanish parish priest, who took pity on him because his features were so different from the local indios.

From Ozal to Engracia

Diligent, cooperative and intelligent, Faisal became close to the priest, who made him a sacristan. It was then that he found in the Christian faith the "milk of human kindness" and forgiveness that had rescued him and his family from poverty. By this grace — gracia — and the blessings of God invoked by the Spanish priest, Faisal was given the name Gracia, later Engracia, before being educated at the parochial school by the civil authorities. So Faisal Ozal became Fruto Engracia, Diamla Ozal became Dimas Engracia, Istam Ozal became Isaac Engracia, and Sahala Ozal became Salvadora Engracia. Already fluent in Chavacano, they became fluent in Spanish as well. This was the next chapter of their lives: from the family name Ozal to the surname Engracia.

Capitan Fruto Engracia

Fruto was humble but intelligent — diligent, sincere, loyal and courageous — and on the priest's recommendation he was accepted into the Spanish army's Guardia Civil after an oral examination. While still a new recruit, bandits2 again attacked Fort Pilar; Fruto and his company repelled them in hand-to-hand combat, and Corporal Fruto was promoted to Sergeant. In the encounters that followed with the "lawless elements"3 his rise was phenomenal: within a year and a half he was Teniente (Lieutenant), and still a bachelor.

Meanwhile the Spanish forts of Misamis Oriental and Occidental, particularly Ozamis, were under constant attack by Moro raiders, and the Commandant and Governor asked Zamboanga for reinforcements. Fruto's company volunteered, and as head of the family he brought his two brothers and his sister with him. Two weeks after their arrival the Fort of Ozamis was attacked by a larger force of juramentados; Fruto and his men fought them on the beach with heavy casualties on both sides, more on the attackers, who withdrew. For this bravery Fruto was promoted to Capitan (Company Commander).

Tall, dark and handsome, the new Capitan was always invited to social gatherings — especially by the Spanish señoritas. At a party hosted by the Governor of the two Misamis provinces he was introduced, as an officer and a gentleman, to a beautiful pure-Spanish lady who had a crush on him. After almost a year they were married in a simple ceremony by the local parish priest: Bruna "Banu" Garcines, daughter of Governor Garcines, became the wife of Capitan Fruto Engracia. They were blessed with five children — Bernandino (Bandin), Norberto (Binto), Eusebio (Sebio), Agustina (Agut) and Isabel (Abe) — and from these five come all the branches of this tree.

The two brothers, Dimas and Isaac, as Spanish employees, were transferred to the Visayas and Luzon and never returned to Mindanao; there are Engracias today in Quezon Province (Mauban, Lucena City, Lucban), the Bicol region, Biliran and other provinces of Luzon who are their heirs. Salvadora, the youngest and only girl, married into the Villa family of Clarin, Misamis Occidental.

1 Zamboanga at that time was still part of the Sultanate of Sulu but colonised by Spain. This must be the reason the place was so often under attack — but the Spanish, for psychological-warfare purposes, branded the attackers as pirates and marauders.

2 Possibly Tausug forces under the Sultanate of Sulu.

3 Resistance and freedom fighters, like the insurrectos in Luzon, were considered "lawless elements" by the Spanish authorities.
